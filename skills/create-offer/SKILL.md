# Create an offer

Turn the user's customer problem into a delivery-ready offer brief. Use their actual constraints; distinguish verified facts from proposed assumptions.

## Find the real outcome

Establish the buyer, the situation that triggers a purchase, the desired observable outcome, the current alternative, and the delivery constraints. Use existing context first. Ask only for gaps that would materially change the offer; if the user wants a draft, mark assumptions and proceed.

Separate the buyer's desired result from the features used to deliver it. A tool, portal, or weekly call is a mechanism, not the result.

## Make value concrete

Evaluate four directions from the value equation: strengthen the desired outcome, increase credible confidence in achieving it, shorten time to first value, and reduce customer effort or sacrifice. Use these as qualitative design prompts, not a numeric valuation formula or evidence that customers will buy.

Map obstacles to possible deliverables. Select the few that address the biggest obstacles and are feasible to deliver. For each, state its customer benefit, owner, time or cost constraint, and dependency. Avoid adding bonuses that complicate understanding without resolving an obstacle.

Make the promise specific enough to check and narrow enough to deliver. Match it to available proof. When evidence is missing, propose a pilot or validation step and use a modest process promise. Do not invent customer results, scarcity, guarantees, testimonials, or savings.

## Produce the brief

Return a buyer-facing offer statement followed by:

- Buyer and buying situation, including who is a poor fit.
- Desired outcome and what the offer actually includes.
- Delivery plan, time to first value, and customer responsibilities.
- Evidence supporting the promise and a separate evidence gap list.
- Price hypothesis, if requested, tied to supplied economics and alternatives.
- One next validation step with a concrete learning objective.

If terms or a guarantee are requested, draft them as proposals with eligibility and limits; don't imply approval to publish or charge. Keep the language plain. Label uncertain details instead of disguising them with confident copy.
